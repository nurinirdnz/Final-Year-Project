# Problem Statement

## Who is affected?

People in Sarawak, especially in towns near the Kalimantan border such as Sri Aman and Kuching, whose air becomes unhealthy during haze seasons. Also the agencies that must respond: the Sarawak Department of Environment, the State Disaster Management Committee and the Fire and Rescue Department (Bernama, 2019; Lu, 2026).

## What exactly is going wrong?

Satellites detect hundreds of fire hotspots a day across Sarawak and Kalimantan, including 1,180 in Kalimantan on 30 August 2026 alone (Malay Mail, 2026). Every hotspot appears as an identical point on a map. There is no automated way to tell lasting fires from brief detections, or to know which fires will send smoke into Sarawak, so haze is only confirmed once monitoring stations record it.

## Why does it matter?

In August 2026, one Sarawak station reached an Air Pollutant Index (API) of 475, well above the hazardous level of 300 and close to the level of 500 at which an emergency declaration is considered (Bernama, 2019; Lu, 2026). Malaysia recorded 1,811 asthma cases between 23 and 29 August 2026, an eightfold increase on the previous week (Lu, 2026). Late warnings leave less time to protect the public.

## Problem statement (3 to 5 sentences)

Hundreds of fire hotspots are detected daily across Sarawak and Kalimantan but each appears as an identical point on a map (Malay Mail, 2026). Agencies have no automated way to separate lasting fires from brief detections, or to link them to Sarawak's air quality. As a result, haze is confirmed only after it arrives, as in August 2026 when one station reached API 475 (Lu, 2026). This leaves little time to warn the public and direct the response, putting people's health at risk.
