# Problem Statement

## Who is affected?

- **People in Sarawak,** especially in towns near the Kalimantan border such as Sri Aman and Kuching, whose air becomes unhealthy during haze seasons. This includes schools and families, who need time to prepare.
- **The agencies that must respond:** the Sarawak Department of Environment, the State Disaster Management Committee and the Fire and Rescue Department (Bernama, 2019; Lu, 2026).

## What exactly is going wrong?

Satellites detect hundreds of fire hotspots a day across Sarawak and Kalimantan. In one week in August 2026, 1,211 hotspots were detected across southern ASEAN, mostly in Kalimantan (Malay Mail, 2026). Three problems get in the way:

1. **Every hotspot looks the same.** Each appears as an identical point on a map, yet many are brief detections rather than lasting fires (Rezainy et al., 2020; Sofan et al., 2022; Syaufina & Sitanggang, 2018). There is no automated way to tell them apart, so officers must judge hundreds of points by eye, which is slow and inconsistent.
2. **Existing forecasts do not use hotspot data.** Fires and wind drive haze in Borneo (Payus et al., 2023), yet the next-day PM10 forecasts reviewed rely on pollutant and weather readings only (Muhammad et al., 2025; Shaziayani, Ahmat, et al., 2022; Shaziayani, Ul-Saufie, et al., 2022), and none ranks hotspots.
3. **Haze is confirmed only after it arrives.** Monitoring measures smoke that is already in the air, so action follows once readings are unhealthy or hazardous.

## Why does it matter?

In August 2026, one Sarawak station reached an Air Pollutant Index (API) of 475, close to the level of 500 at which an emergency declaration is considered (Lu, 2026). Malaysia recorded 1,811 asthma cases between 23 and 29 August 2026, an eightfold increase on the previous week (Lu, 2026). Late warnings leave little time to warn the public, close schools or protect vulnerable groups.

## Problem statement (3 to 5 sentences)

Environment and disaster management agencies, fire and rescue teams, and people living in Sarawak receive satellite hotspot data in which every fire appears as the same dot on a map. Many hotspots are only brief detections and not lasting fires (Rezainy et al., 2020; Sofan et al., 2022; Syaufina & Sitanggang, 2018), and no automatic method separates them, so responders cannot tell which fires matter most for haze. As a result, haze is usually confirmed only after air quality has already fallen, leaving little time to protect the public. For example, in August 2026 a Sarawak station reached an API of 475, the worst air quality since 2019 (Lu, 2026), in the same period that 1,211 hotspots were detected across southern ASEAN in one week (Malay Mail, 2026).
